# Prose Smell Catalog

Use this catalog as a diagnostic matrix. A smell is a reason to inspect a
passage, not a verdict. Report it only when the passage has the stated reader
cost and the proposed change produces a material improvement.

The exceptions below have a burden of proof. Do not clear a smell merely
because a device *can* serve the listed function or because the author intended
it to. Clear it only when the exact passage produces that function effectively.
`It is the style` is never sufficient evidence. When the effect fails, state
that plainly even if the choice was deliberate.

## S1 — Unearned prose

**Trigger:** A sentence or paragraph can be removed without losing action,
tension, character, information, atmosphere, humor, rhythm, emotional
pressure, orientation, or a necessary transition.

**Reader cost:** The passage consumes attention without changing the reading
experience. Repetition and throat-clearing dilute the material that matters.

**Do not flag:** A quiet beat that successfully creates breath, texture,
anticipation, comic timing, or emotional processing. Those functions are real
even when they do not advance plot, but naming one does not prove the beat
achieves it.

**Preferred remedy:** Delete duplicated meaning. If the passage has one useful
element, combine it with a nearby sentence so that element carries more than
one function. Do not demand that every sentence advance action.

## S2 — Generic or abstract experience

**Trigger:** The prose labels an emotion, place, threat, or impression without
giving the particular evidence this POV character would use; or the description
could move to another character, room, monster, or battle with only the nouns
changed.

**Reader cost:** The reader receives a category instead of an experience and
must invent the missing image, behavior, or consequence.

**Do not flag:** Intentional summary used to compress routine or low-value
time; a precise abstract conclusion after adequate evidence; a known setting
that does not need to be rebuilt.

**Preferred remedy:** Replace the generic label with one or two selective
details, an observable choice, a sensory consequence, or a POV-specific
judgment. Specific does not mean exhaustive.

**Common forms:** `terrifying`, `beautiful`, `strange`, `angry`, `sad`,
`advanced technology`, `chaos`, `a sense of`, `somehow`, or `something about`
when the sentence never identifies what creates the impression.

## S3 — Ready-made or inaccurate language

**Trigger:** A cliché, inherited metaphor, mixed image, fashionable phrase, or
ornate comparison arrives without being literally visualized or without
belonging to the POV voice.

**Reader cost:** Familiar wording replaces observation. Inaccurate imagery
forces the reader to stop and reconcile objects or actions that do not fit.

**Do not flag:** A cliché used deliberately in dialogue, comic deflation,
characterization, or a fresh variation that restores a concrete image.

**Preferred remedy:** State the actual observation directly, rebuild the image
from the physical scene, or delete the figure. Do not replace one stock image
with a more decorative stock image.

## S4 — Modifier-dependent wording

**Trigger:** An adjective or adverb supplies information that a stronger noun,
verb, line of dialogue, or action should carry. The highest-value candidates
are tone-explaining dialogue adverbs and stacked intensifiers.

**Reader cost:** The prose interprets itself, weakens the action, or makes the
author audible between character and reader.

**Do not flag:** A modifier that changes meaning precisely, controls degree,
creates rhythm, or is more natural than a showy replacement verb. Never replace
plain `said` with an attention-seeking speech verb merely to remove an adverb.

**Preferred remedy:** First test deletion. If meaning disappears, choose a more
exact noun, verb, action beat, or line—not automatically a more colorful word.

## S5 — Obscured agency or emphasis

**Trigger:** Passive construction, nominalization, delayed subject, or clause
order makes it unnecessarily hard to tell who acted, what happened, or what the
sentence wants emphasized.

**Reader cost:** Action loses force or accountability; the reader must unpack
the grammar before imagining the event.

**Do not flag:** Passive voice that correctly foregrounds the recipient,
conceals an unknown actor, preserves POV uncertainty, connects to the previous
sentence's topic, or improves rhythm. Forms of `be` are not automatically
passive.

**Preferred remedy:** Put the relevant actor near a concrete verb, place
related words together, and move the intended emphasis to the beginning or end.
Choose focus deliberately rather than converting every passive mechanically.

## S6 — Evidence followed by explanation

**Trigger:** The prose shows an emotion, motive, joke, threat, relationship, or
meaning through action/dialogue/image, then names it, paraphrases it, or
explains why the reader should interpret it that way.

**Reader cost:** The second beat blunts the first and signals distrust in the
reader. Emotional moments often become melodramatic because they land twice.

**Do not flag:** The explanation changes, contradicts, or complicates the
evidence; the POV interpretation is itself characterization; or clarity would
otherwise fail because the evidence is intentionally ambiguous.

**Preferred remedy:** Delete the translation and let the evidence stand. If the
interpretation matters as character thought, make it partial, biased, or
consequential rather than a neutral explanation.

## S7 — Reader-serving perception

**Trigger:** The POV character notices, remembers, asks about, or mentally
defines something mainly because the reader needs information, despite having
no present reason to attend to it.

**Reader cost:** The narrator's hand becomes visible and the character briefly
behaves like a tour guide.

**Do not flag:** The detail affects the character's goal, threat assessment,
relationship, plan, or sensory attention in the moment; novelty gives the POV a
real reason to inspect it.

**Preferred remedy:** Tie the information to a current decision or
consequence, move it to a moment when it becomes relevant, or omit it until the
reader needs it. Preserve the character's knowledge limits.

## S8 — Author-driven exposition

**Trigger:** Backstory or worldbuilding arrives as a block because the author
wants the fact recorded, not because the scene has created a question,
consequence, conflict, choice, or object that makes the information useful now.

**Reader cost:** Present pressure pauses while the book lectures. Information
is harder to retain because it has no active hook.

**Do not flag:** Deliberately paced political, reflective, or worldbuilding
scenes that pay for their length through character, tension, discovery, or
atmosphere. Afela explicitly allows non-action scenes to breathe.

**Preferred remedy:** Keep only what changes the current read; ground the
concept in an object, interface behavior, action, cost, or misunderstanding;
redistribute later facts to the moments when they acquire consequences.

## S9 — Artificial withholding

**Trigger:** Narration hides information the POV character is actively using or
would naturally think about, solely so the author can reveal it later. Vague
substitutes such as `the thing`, `what had happened`, or `the plan` often mark
the seam.

**Reader cost:** Suspense becomes a contest between author and reader instead
of uncertainty inside the story. The eventual reveal can feel dishonest.

**Do not flag:** The POV genuinely lacks the information, refuses to confront
it, has no reason to formulate it, or the prose clearly dramatizes avoidance as
character behavior.

**Preferred remedy:** Give the reader the known fact and relocate suspense to
its outcome, cost, interpretation, or whether the plan will work.

## S10 — Dialogue that needs authorial support

**Trigger:** Dialogue requires an adverb or explanatory beat to supply tone;
characters state shared knowledge for the reader; voices converge on the same
polished diction; or a line fills air without characterization, information,
pressure, misdirection, or rhythm.

**Reader cost:** Characters become delivery mechanisms and subtext disappears.

**Do not flag:** Deliberate repetition, evasion, ritual language, small talk
used as pressure, or a plain exchange whose naturalness is the point.

**Preferred remedy:** Strengthen the line's word choice or subtext, attach the
information to disagreement or action, cut the support sentence, or delete the
line. Keep dialogue tags plain unless an unusual verb conveys information not
present in the speech.

## S11 — Mechanical cadence or false landing

**Trigger:** Several consecutive sentences share length and construction;
paragraphs repeatedly end with compact dramatic declarations; fragment stacks,
tricolons, conjunction chains, or emphatic tail clauses become the default
rhythm rather than a chosen effect.

**Reader cost:** Cadence becomes predictable. Repeated punch lines flatten real
emphasis, and ordinary beats masquerade as climaxes.

**Do not flag:** A short run that accelerates action, creates comedy, imitates a
character's mental state, or builds toward a specific impact. One dramatic
ending is not a pattern.

**Preferred remedy:** Read the span aloud; vary clause shape and sentence
length; combine or split only where meaning supports it; delete false summary
lines; reserve fragments and emphatic endings for actual turns.

## S12 — Misframed or unchanged scene

**Trigger:** A scene begins before meaningful pressure, continues after its
consequence, lacks a discernible immediate want, or exits with no perceptible
change in action, knowledge, relationship, danger, emotional position, or
future choice.

**Reader cost:** Even polished prose feels static because the reader cannot
measure movement or understand why this interval was dramatized.

**Do not flag:** Brief connective tissue, deliberate stasis that builds dread,
a scene whose change is subtle but behaviorally visible, or a reflective sequel
that produces a decision.

**Preferred remedy:** Enter nearer the pressure; cut the post-consequence tail;
make the immediate want legible; or sharpen an already authorized consequence
so the exit state registers. If the scene needs a new event, route the decision
to the author or `pacing-review` rather than inventing one.

## S13 — Unearned causal payoff

**Trigger:** A climax, solution, deduction, or emotional reversal depends on an
unseeded capability, cost-free mechanic, missing premise, or behavior the prose
has not made credible.

**Reader cost:** The result feels authored into existence instead of caused by
the story.

**Do not flag:** The setup exists elsewhere in the manuscript or codex, the
surprise is meant to be explained later without resolving the current conflict,
or the POV plausibly misreads what occurred.

**Preferred remedy:** This is usually not a line edit. Identify the exact
missing setup or visible cost and refer the factual check to
`continuity-reviewer` and the setup/payoff weighting to `pacing-review`. Draft a
replacement only when existing authorized facts already support a clearer
sentence.

## Calibration basis

The catalog synthesizes these durable ideas:

- William Strunk's composition advice: organize around units of thought,
  prefer concrete language, remove redundancy, keep related words together,
  vary sentence structures, and place emphasis deliberately.
- Stephen King's *On Writing*: optimize for immersion and readable language;
  treat conspicuous adverbs and passive constructions as revision prompts;
  use selective description, character-revealing dialogue, an ideal reader,
  sustained practice, and revision distance.
- George Orwell's prose test: resist stale imagery, inflated abstraction,
  ready-made phrases, obscured agency, and words chosen before meaning.
- Kurt Vonnegut's fiction heuristics: respect the reader's time, give
  characters wants, make sentences perform work, and dramatize pressure that
  reveals character.
- Ursula K. Le Guin's craft emphasis: grammar, viewpoint, sentence construction,
  and sound are flexible tools; read prose aloud and learn their effects rather
  than enforcing bans.

Use these sources as lenses, not authorities that outrank the story. Modern
usage guidance and linguistic criticism show why blanket prohibitions against
passive voice or adverbs are unreliable. That nuance is not permission to go
easy on failed prose. The operative questions are always: **what does this
choice actually do here, and does another choice do it better?**
