# Sanderson Review

Review what the story promises, how its chapters or scenes supply felt progress, what it pays off, and whether intended beats land in function rather than merely appearing by keyword. This is the project's architecture-level Sanderson audit; it works before or after prose exists.

## Required references

Always read these bundled files completely before reviewing:

- `references/framework.md` — definitions, beat-status rules, and diagnostic thresholds.
- `references/report-template.md` — required report structure and actionable-finding format.

Read `references/literature-examples.md` only when a material finding would
genuinely benefit from a published craft comparison. If you open it, read it
completely and follow its attribution rules. A review does not need an analogy.

Read `codex/outline/Sanderson Method — {{NOVEL_TITLE}} Working Guide.md` completely. It is the project-specific application of the course framework.

## Scope and authority

- Produce a review report only. Do not change chapter prose, outlines, summaries, codex facts, or discovery flags.
- Treat the manuscript as fact, the codex as established support, and outlines as intended-but-revisable plans.
- Surface substantive outline/manuscript or canon conflicts as decisions. Never silently decide that either side should change.
- Review structure and delivery. Leave sentence craft to `prose-review`, rate-of-delivery problems to `pacing-review`, grammar and punctuation mechanics to `proofread`, and factual continuity to `continuity-reviewer`, except where those issues prevent a promise, beat, or payoff from functioning.
- A slow domestic, humorous, atmospheric, or reflective scene may be valid progress when it fulfills a deliberate tone or relationship promise. Do not demand action from every scene.

## Choose the scope

Use the narrowest mode that answers the request:

- **Drafted chapter:** Review the prose scene by scene against its plan.
- **Chapter outline:** Audit draft readiness, expected scene changes, escalation, earned mechanics, and the chapter's handoff before prose exists.
- **Arc:** Audit chapter-level promise renewal, visible progress, escalation, turning points, and payoffs across the arc.
- **Plot thread:** Trace one promise from setup through progress signposts to payoff and consequence.
- **Whole book:** Audit the macro contract, major arcs, climax causality, character movement, system progression, and interconnection.

For outline-only work, replace manuscript evidence with exact outline anchors. Mark judgments as **planned**, not landed. Never imply that an outlined beat already works on the page.

## Gather the relevant context

For a drafted chapter or chapter outline, read:

1. `codex/00 Index.md`, `codex/Genre.md`, `codex/Writing Style.md`, and `codex/Editing Workflow.md`.
2. The complete chapter manuscript when it exists.
3. That chapter's `Outline.md` and `Summary.md`.
4. The previous chapter's `Summary.md`; read the next chapter's planned summary or arc entry when available to understand the exit handoff.
5. The relevant `codex/outline/Arc ...` file.
6. Every thread file named by the outline's `Advances`, `Purpose`, or beat codes.
7. Any mechanic, character, or location note required to judge a planned solution, cost, reveal, or relationship turn.

Do not read the whole vault indiscriminately. Follow the outline's direct links and use the indexes to resolve only what the review needs.

For an arc, thread, or whole-book review, also read `codex/outline/00 Outline Index.md`, the complete files in scope, and the smallest set of linked character, mechanic, mystery, and progression notes needed to test causality. For a whole-book review, include the macro structure, all arc plans, active plot threads, and the progression tracker if one exists. Do not open large future-note files unless a direct dependency requires them.

If only pasted text is available, perform the intrinsic review, clearly mark which plan/canon verification was unavailable, and never invent intended beats.

## Review workflow

### 1. Establish the intended contract

Before judging scenes, write a private one-paragraph statement of:

- the chapter's purpose;
- its expected entry and exit state;
- the promises it renews or creates;
- the exact arc/thread beats it is meant to advance;
- the small payoff expected inside this chapter;
- the larger payoff it prepares.

Separate explicit outline requirements from your interpretation. Include tone, external plot, internal change, relationship, mystery, and LitRPG/progression promises when relevant.

### 2. Read the complete scope

Do not flag while sampling. Read through the end of the chapter, arc, thread, or book plan before judging whether an early promise progresses or pays off.

### 3. Build the progression map

For a drafted chapter, split on scene breaks, POV changes, meaningful time/place cuts, or a new immediate goal. For an outline, arc, or thread, use the planned scenes, chapters, or beats as the rows. For each unit identify:

- POV and location;
- immediate desire or goal;
- entry state;
- resistance, complication, or dilemma;
- turn, decision, disaster, revelation, or relationship change;
- exit state;
- progress type;
- outline/thread beat served;
- promise renewed or payoff prepared/delivered.

Use the progression-map format in `references/report-template.md`. A beat code is an intent label, not evidence of reader-visible progress.

### 4. Verify beats functionally

List every expected beat. For drafted prose, assign exactly one status from
`references/framework.md`:

- `LANDED`
- `PARTIAL`
- `MISSING`
- `ALTERED`

For an undrafted outline or plan, use:

- `PLANNED` — the required event, function, and resulting state are specified;
- `PARTIAL` — part of the function is planned, but a necessary change, cost,
  setup, or consequence is absent;
- `ABSENT` — no planned unit provides the required function;
- `CONFLICT` — the plan contradicts canon, another planned beat, a resource
  state, or its required exit state.

An event is not enough. A beat lands only when the manuscript delivers its intended narrative function and the reader can perceive the change. Quote short exact evidence for `LANDED` and `PARTIAL`; give the nearest relevant anchor for `MISSING` and `ALTERED`.

For outline statuses, cite the exact planned anchor. Never call a planned beat
`LANDED`, and do not predict that prose execution will succeed merely because
the plan is sound.

Track meaningful unplanned beats separately. Do not treat harmless connective tissue as deviation.

### 5. Audit promise, progress, and payoff

Apply the criteria in `references/framework.md` at both levels:

- **Chapter level:** Does the opening establish or renew a clear expectation? Does the middle visibly change its likelihood, cost, meaning, or route? Does the ending answer a small promise or create a deliberate handoff?
- **Unit level:** What expectation carries the reader into the scene/chapter/beat, what changes, and what consequence pulls forward?

Check all relevant progress channels: external goal, information, relationship, internal belief, capability/mastery, setting/system understanding, and tone.

### 6. Test character movement, escalation, and earned solutions

For each major POV or arc character, test:

- **Motivation, personality, and values:** Are they distinct, under pressure, and changing through choices?
- **Proactivity:** Do their decisions cause important outcomes?
- **Relatability:** Is there a legible desire, vulnerability, limitation, or cost?
- **Capability:** Is competence demonstrated through specific action rather than labels?
- **Movement:** Does the scope change what they want, believe, risk, can do, or refuse to do?

For action, investigation, persuasion, or planning sequences, identify the attempt ladder. Flag repeated attempts that do not become harder, smarter, costlier, or more revealing.

For every solution or reveal, verify:

- the necessary rule or clue was established;
- limitations and costs remain active;
- the character's decision causes the result;
- the payoff uses the chapter's or book's thematic core;
- no new capability appears only when needed.

Trace important resources and limitations from opening state through gains, spends, damage, and closing state. Apply this to heat, ammunition, mass, injury, information, social leverage, skills, levels, and other story-specific currencies.

### 7. Test worldbuilding and progression

Check whether plot, character, and setting solve one another instead of running as separate tracks:

- expand an established element before adding another;
- make new mechanics affect plot, character choice, and the lived setting;
- teach limitations, costs, and reader-visible behavior before a mechanic solves a major problem;
- ground unfamiliar abstractions in a concrete POV need, action, comparison, or consequence;
- reveal only the portion of the iceberg the current story needs;
- ensure LitRPG progression changes tactics, options, identity, pressure, or cost rather than merely increasing numbers.

### 8. Test opposition and choice

Verify that opposition attacks the chosen method, not merely the objective. Major choices need credible alternatives, sacrifice, or irreversible consequence. A climax should grow from a changed combination of established tools, relationships, understanding, and accepted costs.

### 9. Draft actionable findings

Report only material findings. Use `BLOCKER` when the current plan cannot earn its climax or required exit state without a new decision, setup, capability, or resource. Use `IMPORTANT` when a required beat is missing/altered, progress is structurally absent, a promise is false or broken, a solution is unearned, or the scope exits in the wrong state. Use `OPPORTUNITY` when the function exists but would benefit from clearer signposting, escalation, setup, reaction, interconnection, or thematic alignment. For drafted-chapter compatibility, `SUGGESTION` is accepted as an alias for `OPPORTUNITY`.

Each finding must include:

- exact location and original anchor;
- diagnosis tied to promise, progress, payoff, beat, or mechanic;
- the outline/codex expectation;
- a concrete proposed change;
- an exact replacement for a local fix, or a transformation rule plus a worked example for a structural fix;
- one literature comparison only when it genuinely clarifies the fix.

For outline, arc, thread, and whole-book findings, the concrete change should
be an exact beat insertion, deletion, reorder, split, merge, or transformation
rule with the required resulting state. Do not draft manuscript prose unless
the user specifically asks for an illustrative example.

Do not produce a finding solely to use a literary example.

### 10. Use literature comparisons responsibly

Choose from `references/literature-examples.md` by craft function, not prestige or surface similarity.

- Write `Comparable craft move:` followed by the title, author, spoiler scope, and a concise paraphrase.
- Explain the structural similarity and the actionable difference for {{NOVEL_TITLE}}.
- Never write as Sanderson, say “I did this,” invent author commentary, or imply personal access to the author.
- Paraphrase by default. Do not quote novels. Quote an official lecture or author annotation only when necessary, link it, and keep the quotation under 15 words.
- Mark spoilers before the example.
- If no verified comparison fits, omit the analogy rather than fabricate one.

### 11. Save the report

Save the report in the vault-level `reviews/` folder:

`<Scope Name> - sanderson-review-<YYYY-MM-DD>.md`

If the user explicitly requests an in-chat review only, return the same structure without saving.

Close with 2–4 precise strengths: promises, progress signals, beat landings, or payoffs already working. Avoid generic praise.

Use `references/report-template.md` for drafted chapters. For outline, arc, thread, or whole-book work, adapt its scene map into a chapter/beat progression map and add:

- **Draft Readiness:** READY / READY WITH REVISIONS / NOT READY;
- **Character Movement:** motivation/personality/values and proactive/relatable/capable changes;
- **Worldbuilding, Mechanics, and Progression:** established element expanded, limitations/costs, resource ledger, and interconnection;
- **Decisions Needed:** questions that require author choice rather than reviewer invention.

## Final quality check

Before handing off, verify:

- every outline beat appears in the beat matrix;
- every scene, chapter, or planned unit appears in the progression map;
- every `MISSING`, `ALTERED`, `ABSENT`, or `CONFLICT` beat has an actionable finding or an explicit decision note;
- every proposed addition respects POV, voice, mechanics, and canon;
- every literature comparison is accurate, relevant, spoiler-labelled, and paraphrased;
- no recommendation quietly changes a later outlined beat;
- the report, not the manuscript, is the only file created or changed.

Do not add this reviewer to `chapter-cycle`. In that workflow, `pacing-review` owns reader-visible progress and `continuity-reviewer` owns capability/resource legality; this skill remains a deliberate architecture audit invoked by the user.
